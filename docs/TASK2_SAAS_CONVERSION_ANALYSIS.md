# Task 2: SaaS Conversion Analysis

## The question
A SaaS platform focused on providing Family Office Intelligence to a targeted audience is
only converting 3% of free accounts to paying users. The founders want to increase MRR. How
would you improve this free-trial-to-paid conversion rate?

## Answer

The 3% conversion problem for a family office intelligence product is not a funnel problem,
and the fastest way to fail it is to reach for the standard SaaS playbook: onboarding emails,
urgency timers, discounts, paywall tweaks, better CTAs. Those optimize the rate at which
people bounce off a product whose value has not landed. For this product, conversion is
downstream of one thing: did the trial hand the user a piece of reachable intelligence they
could not get anywhere else, and was it correct? A 3% rate means roughly 97% of trialers never
reached that moment, so I fix the moment, not the cosmetics.

I start by diagnosing before prescribing. I instrument the funnel to find the difference
between the 3% who pay and the 97% who do not. My hypothesis is that payers are users who ran
a query in their own vertical or geography and got a verified, reachable decision maker at a
single family office, which is the record they cannot buy elsewhere. I would confirm which of
three gaps is killing conversion: coverage (no record in their niche), actionability (a record
exists but the contact is blank), or trust (they got a hit but could not tell it was real). You
cannot fix the number without knowing which of those is true.

From there, the core lever is the moat, aimed rather than scaled blindly. The differentiated
value is exactly what is not on the web: single family offices reachable only through
relationships, networks, offline sessions, and word of mouth. That is worth investing in. But I
would not broadly multiply sourcing, because the founders want MRR, which is fast and
measurable, and untargeted sourcing is slow and does not touch the conversion mechanic. Instead
I deepen coverage in the specific verticals and regions our actual trialers are searching, so
the trial reliably lands a hit for them. Targeted depth converts; raw volume does not.

Then I engineer the first success inside the trial itself. Onboarding captures the user's
target (sector, geography, ticket size), and the trial guarantees at least one verified,
contactable single family office principal in that target, shown with its provenance so the hit
is trusted, not just displayed. A fund manager pays for correct and reachable, never for a row
in a table.

Packaging follows the value unit, which is the actionable intelligence record. The free tier
should prove the quality but meter the depth (verified contacts, exports, dated signals). A 3%
rate can also signal a price or free-tier mismatch, which I would A/B test, but only after the
value moment works, because pricing a product nobody has felt value from just rearranges deck
chairs.

Finally, this is an MRR question, not a one-time-list question, so retention has to be built
in. The dated signals (recent hires, fund commitments, deals) are what make the subscription
worth keeping. A static contact list churns after the first month; a live view of who is active
this quarter is what sustains recurring revenue.

On confidence, deliberately: I will not attach a number like "88% sure of 20 to 25% next
quarter," because unfalsifiable precision is exactly the failure this domain punishes. My
defensible claim is that coverage depth plus a guaranteed first hit in the user's own niche is
the highest leverage lever. I would pilot it on one vertical, measure the activation-to-paid
rate, and let the data set the target. I expect a meaningful lift, and I would rather earn the
number than assert it.

## The reasoning in short
Conversion follows value rather than the reverse, especially for a data product, so make the
value undeniable first and optimize the mechanics second. The moat instinct (offline-sourced
single family office data) is correct; I only pointed it at the segments trialers actually
search, because that is what moves conversion. And swapping a precise multiple-x promise for a
lever I can pilot and measure is the single change that turns this from an overclaim into a
defensible decision.
